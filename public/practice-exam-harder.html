Inference — propositional & predicate

Question 1

10 points
(a)[4] Translate into predicate logic: "Every user who trusts a website that is not secure will eventually be scammed." Use U(x): x is a user, W(y): y is a website, Trusts(x,y), Secure(y), Scammed(x), over a shared domain of people and websites.
(b)[6] Use a chain of logical equivalences — naming every law — to simplify ¬((p → q) ∧ (¬p → r)) down to a disjunction of two conjunctions of literals.

Question 2

8 points

Given the premises below, derive s. Label the rule used at every step — your derivation must use Resolution at least once.

1. p ∨ r   2. ¬p ∨ q   3. ¬r   4. ¬q ∨ s

Question 3

6 points

Negate the statement below and use De Morgan's laws for quantifiers to push the negation all the way inward, past all three quantifiers and the conditional:

∀x ∃y ∀z ( P(x,y) → Q(y,z) )

Proof techniques

Question 4

8 points

Prove: for integers a and b, if a²(b² − 2b) is odd, then both a and b are odd. (Choose whichever proof method is most efficient — state which method you're using.)

Question 5

8 points

Prove the biconditional: for an integer n, n is even ⟺ n² − 2n is even. You will need two different proof techniques, one per direction — name each.

Functions & countability of sets

Question 6

8 points

Prove: if A and B are both countably infinite sets, then A ∪ B is countable. (General proof — do not assume a specific A or B.)

Question 7

6 points

Let f: A → B and g: B → C both be injective. Prove that g ∘ f is injective.

Sequences & summations

Question 8

10 points

A sequence satisfies a₁ = 3, aₙ = 2aₙ₋₁ + 1 for n ≥ 2. Find a closed formula for aₙ. (Hint: consider the substitution bₙ = aₙ + 1 before iterating.)

Question 9

6 points

Evaluate Σ (k=2 to n) 1 / (k(k−1)) in closed form. (Hint: rewrite each term as a difference of two fractions, then telescope.)

Induction & recursion

Question 10

14 points

Use strong induction to prove that every integer n ≥ 8 can be written as n = 3a + 5b for some nonnegative integers a, b. State the basis step, inductive hypothesis, and inductive step explicitly.

Question 11

10 points

The set P of balanced-parenthesis strings is defined recursively:

Basis: () ∈ P.
Recursive: if w ∈ P, then ()w ∈ P, (w) ∈ P, and w() ∈ P.

Use structural induction to prove that every string in P has even length.

Relations

Question 12

12 points
(a)[6] Let R = {(a,b) ∈ ℤ×ℤ : 4 | (a − b)}. Prove, in general (not by listing pairs), that R is an equivalence relation on ℤ.
(b)[3] List the equivalence classes of R.
(c)[3] Let B = {1, 2, 3, 6, 12} ordered by "divides." List every covering relation (i.e. every edge that would appear in the Hasse diagram of this poset).
Counting techniques

Question 13

14 points
(a)[5] Give a combinatorial (not algebraic) proof of Pascal's Identity: C(n,r) = C(n−1,r−1) + C(n−1,r), for general n and r.
(b)[5] How many ways can 10 identical candies be distributed among 4 distinct children so that every child gets at least one candy?
(c)[4] Show that among any 100 integers, there exist two whose difference is divisible by 11. Name the principle you use.
Basics of graphs

Question 14

16 points
(a)[4] A connected simple graph has 7 vertices with degree sequence (4,4,3,3,2,2,2). Find the number of edges, and state whether the graph has an Euler circuit, an Euler path, or neither — justify by counting odd-degree vertices.
(b)[6] Determine whether G and H below are isomorphic. If yes, exhibit an explicit vertex bijection and verify it preserves every edge; if no, name an invariant that rules it out.
G: vertices {1,2,3,4,5}, edges {(1,2),(2,3),(3,4),(4,5),(5,1),(1,3)}
H: vertices {a,b,c,d,e}, edges {(a,b),(b,c),(c,d),(d,e),(e,a),(b,d)}
(c)[6] For graph G in part (b): compute every vertex's degree. Does G have an Euler circuit? Does G have a Hamilton circuit — if so, exhibit one.
