Question 1 · Inference

15 points
(a)[3] Translate into predicate logic over the domain of integers: "A number is divisible by 6 only if it is divisible by both 2 and 3." Use predicates D6(x), D2(x), D3(x).
(b)[4] Use a series of logical equivalences — naming each law used — to show that ¬(p → (q ∧ r)) ≡ p ∧ (¬q ∨ ¬r).
(c)[4] Determine whether each statement is True or False. Justify with an example or counterexample.
  1. ∃x ∈ ℤ (x³ = −x)
  2. ∀x ∈ ℝ⁺ (⌊x⌋ < x)
(d)[4] Given the premises below, use rules of inference to derive q. Label the rule used at each step.
1. p → ¬r   2. r ∨ s   3. ¬s ∨ q   4. p

Question 2 · Proof Techniques

12 points
(a)[6] Prove by contraposition: for an integer n, if n² − 6n + 5 is even, then n is odd.
(b)[6] Prove by contradiction that there is no smallest positive rational number.

Question 3 · Functions & Countability

13 points
(a)[4] Let f: ℤ → ℤ, f(n) = 3n − 2. Prove f is injective. Is f surjective onto ℤ? Justify.
(b)[4] Let g: ℝ → ℝ, g(x) = x³. Prove g is bijective and state g⁻¹.
(c)[5] Show that the set of positive odd integers is countably infinite by exhibiting an explicit bijection with ℤ⁺, and prove your function is a bijection.

Question 4 · Sequences & Summations

10 points
(a)[5] A sequence satisfies a₁ = 4, aₙ = aₙ₋₁ + 5 for n ≥ 2. Use iteration (forward or backward substitution) to conjecture a closed formula for aₙ.
(b)[5] Evaluate Σ (i=1 to 6) (3i − 1) two ways: (i) by direct expansion, (ii) using the standard summation formulas. Confirm the two agree.

Question 5 · Induction

14 points

Use mathematical induction to prove that for every positive integer n:

1·1! + 2·2! + 3·3! + ⋯ + n·n! = (n + 1)! − 1

State the basis step, the inductive hypothesis, and the inductive step explicitly.

Question 6 · Relations

12 points

Let A = {1, 2, 3, 4} and define R = {(a,b) | a,b ∈ A, a ≡ b (mod 2)}.

(a)[6] List the ordered pairs of R. Determine, with justification, whether R is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive.
(b)[6] Is R an equivalence relation? If so, list its equivalence classes on A. Separately, describe the partition that "a ≡ b (mod 2)" induces on all of ℤ.

Question 7 · Counting

14 points
(a)[4] A 4-character password has two uppercase letters followed by two digits; letters may not repeat each other, and digits may not repeat each other. How many such passwords exist?
(b)[4] A committee of 5 is chosen from 7 men and 6 women, and must contain exactly 3 men and 2 women. How many committees are possible?
(c)[3] Among any 50 integers, show that at least some number of them must leave the same remainder when divided by 12. State that number and name the principle used.
(d)[3] Use Pascal's Identity to write C(6,2) as a sum of two binomial coefficients with upper index 5, then verify the numerical value.

Question 8 · Graphs

14 points
(a)[4] A simple graph has 6 vertices with degree sequence 3, 3, 3, 3, 2, 2. Verify this is consistent with the Handshaking Theorem and state the number of edges.
(b)[5] Graph G has vertices {a,b,c,d,e} and edges {(a,b),(b,c),(c,d),(d,e),(e,a),(a,c)}. Compute every vertex's degree, then determine whether G has an Euler circuit, an Euler path, or neither — name the theorem you use, and give the path/circuit if one exists.
(c)[5] Graph G has 5 vertices, 6 edges, and degree sequence (3,3,2,2,2). Graph H has 5 vertices, 6 edges, and degree sequence (3,3,3,2,1). Are G and H necessarily isomorphic? Could they be? Justify precisely.
